## Summary
An index fund is a type of mutual fund or ETF designed to track a market index (such as the S&P 500 or a total stock market index) rather than attempting to outperform the market through active stock selection.

## Key points
- **Passive strategy:** The fund holds securities that mirror the index, with limited trading compared to actively managed funds.
- **Lower average costs:** Because there is no active stock-picking team making frequent trades, expense ratios are typically lower than actively managed funds.
- **Broad diversification:** A single index fund can provide exposure to hundreds or even thousands of securities.
- **Market-matching returns:** Investors generally receive the return of the index, minus fees and minor tracking differences.
- **Transparent holdings:** Index composition is typically publicly available and rules-based.

## Common indexes
- **S&P 500:** Large-cap U.S. companies.
- **Total stock market:** Broad exposure across large-, mid-, and small-cap U.S. stocks.
- **Total bond market:** Broad exposure to U.S. investment-grade bonds.
- **International indexes:** Developed and emerging market stocks outside the U.S.

## Common misconceptions
- **"Index funds eliminate risk."** No. They reduce company-specific risk through diversification but still carry overall market risk.
- **"Index funds always outperform active funds."** Not always. Some active funds outperform in certain periods, though many underperform after fees over long horizons.

## Example
If you invest in an S&P 500 index fund, you effectively own small portions of 500 large U.S. companies. If the overall U.S. large-cap market rises, your fund generally rises as well—minus the fund’s expense ratio.
