
Title: Rebalancing
Source: SEC Investor.gov
URL: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/asset-allocation
AsOf: 2026-02-15

## Summary
Rebalancing is the process of adjusting a portfolio back to its target asset allocation by selling assets that have grown beyond their target weight and buying those that have fallen below it.

## Key points
- **Why it matters:** Over time, investments that perform well can grow to represent a larger portion of your portfolio, potentially increasing overall risk beyond your original plan.
- **Restores intended risk level:** Rebalancing helps maintain the risk profile you originally chose.
- **Common methods:** Investors may rebalance on a fixed schedule (e.g., annually), when allocations drift beyond a threshold (e.g., 5%), or a combination of both.
- **Tax considerations:** Rebalancing in taxable accounts can trigger capital gains taxes. Using tax-advantaged accounts (such as 401(k)s or IRAs) or directing new contributions to underweighted assets may reduce taxable events.
- **Requires discipline:** Rebalancing often means selling assets that have recently performed well and buying those that have underperformed.

## Common misconceptions
- **"Rebalancing increases returns."** Not necessarily. Its primary goal is risk control, not maximizing returns.
- **"Rebalancing should be done constantly."** Frequent rebalancing can increase transaction costs and taxes without significant benefit.

## Example
Suppose your target allocation is 60% stocks and 40% bonds. After a strong stock market year, your portfolio becomes 70% stocks and 30% bonds. Rebalancing would involve selling some stocks and buying bonds to return to the 60/40 target allocation.
